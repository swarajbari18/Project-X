## Status

Worked 2026-09-14. This is a conversation-and-discoveries record, not a decision record. It was triggered by the viral "Morning Bathrobe Rant: Rethinking Harnesses" video by Robert C. ## The problem statement (Swaraj's, kept as the spine)

Swaraj opened the session from a video he had just watched and from his own prior build, and asked for research plus a complete analysis. His framing, kept close to raw:

- **The video.** A software-engineering book author built an elaborate harness — gates, planners, validators, eval suites — until "nothing was failing" and he could predict exactly what the agent would do next. Then he ran the same queries on a bare single-loop (ReAct-style) agent on a frontier model and the answers came back so much better that it broke him, and he went on a recorded rant that "harness engineering is useless": it raises determinism, not quality.
- **The prior build.** Swaraj's own system had precise architecture, orchestration, and non-LLM execution. Given a query he could predict exactly which tool would fire, in what order, and it did. The output was *correct* — it did what was asked — and still scored about 40/100 on quality. A few prompt changes made by a colleague took quality to 95/100, but the system also became less deterministic, more like a React agent. That trade is the whole problem.
- **The current project's constraint.** Determinism and correctness are the first priority (harness-owned); quality is the second, and the plan for it is fine-tuning plus a memory layer that injects content from the knowledge and memory graph at appropriate steps. The model floor is deliberately modest — a Gemma ~26B-class model on Cloudflare — because frontier providers regress models, and batch and inference tiers do not perform at the same level of intelligence. So quality has to come from the loop and context management, not from buying a bigger model.
- **The ask.** Research the quality-of-output question (not determinism) across the agent ecosystem — papers, GitHub reports, Hermes-agent material — and the social signal: what devs on X/Twitter are actually saying about harness agents vs simple loops. Then answer: *why is this true? what is really happening when a harness executes perfectly and still answers badly? which of Swaraj's derived concepts hold up against evidence? and what is it that this project is doing that the current "harness engineering" conversation is not — the thing we have not been able to articulate?*
- **The medium.** Explicitly requested: use the Chrome MCP to put a detailed research prompt into Grok (which has direct X/Twitter search access) for the social-signal leg, TinyFish for the web legs, and our own search tools alongside.

## The central claim — correctness of execution and quality of output are different dimensions

The contradiction Swaraj kept returning to — *why did the system that executed perfectly score 40/100, and why did loosening it to a React shape lift quality to 95?* — resolves cleanly once the two dimensions are separated. His own analogy held: correctness of execution and quality of output relate like computer science engineering and software engineering — closely related, but different disciplines.

**Dimension 1: determinism / correctness of execution.** Does the specified thing happen, repeatably, in the specified order, within the specified authority? This is machine-checkable, it is what the harness owns, and it is what every harness-only benchmark lift measures (the community's repeated scoreboard: same model, better loop/middleware → Terminal-Bench 52.8 → 66.5, LangChain / gpt-5.2-codex). Determinism is a property of the *machine*.

**Dimension 2: quality of output.** Is the artifact the *right* thing to have made — did it answer what the human actually meant? This is not machine-checkable by the same means, it is a property of the *artifact*, and it is dominated by model accuracy of execution, by what "done" is specified to look like, and by the freshness and signal-to-noise of the context the model reasons from. A perfect compiler does not produce good software; a perfect harness does not produce good answers.

Swaraj's prior build proves the split empirically: three verification layers (plan validity, business correctness, response grounding) all passed while the answer was still poor — exactly the known defect recorded in the study file for TeleChat ("none asks 'did we answer what the human actually meant?'… Structural validity ≠ intent fidelity"). The same failure is now the cleanest documented case in the wild: Oracle's refund-agent incident (Oracle Developers blog, Sep 3 2026) — "closes 140 tickets… Forty-one of them never reached the payments API. The model wrote 'refund issued' because that is what the end of a refund conversation looks like." Perfect process fidelity — the *shape* of a completed interaction — with zero output accuracy. Where there is no compiler for "refund actually hit the payments API," the agent optimizes the shape of a completed conversation instead. That is precision without accuracy, measured in production.

The 40→95 jump, then, was not the colleague adding quality; it was the colleague *removing constraints that were converting the model's own accuracy into a corridor* — the 1500-line planner, the fixed output template, the system-knowledge dumps — and letting the model's per-step accuracy reach the answer. Quality did not come from the harness. It came from what the harness had been suppressing.

## Why a simple loop with a frontier model produces high quality (the mechanism)

The question underneath the rant — *why does a bare loop on a frontier model answer so well?* — decomposes into four concrete causes. All four came out of the research with named sources; none of them is magic, and together they explain both Uncle Bob's result and Swaraj's 40→95.

**1. The "simple loop" is itself a harness — the loop as context.** The loop (tool → observe → reflect → next action) lets the model reason from *execution evidence*: the actual file, the actual error, the actual state, at the moment it decides. Swaraj's old planner told it everything in advance in one giant static lump. Replacing the planner with a loop did not remove the harness — it moved the intelligence from *static upfront prediction* into *dynamic per-step context*. That is context engineering happening for free, and it is the real lesson of the video: it lands exactly on the three-layer split the study file already names (context engineering / harness engineering / loop engineering). Birgitta Böckeler's framing (Thoughtworks, martinfowler.com, Apr 2026) says the same thing from the user's side: guides (feedforward) and sensors (feedback), and the coding harness is partly pre-built into the agent itself.

**2. Frontier models have higher accuracy of execution, so they need less process control.** This is the "uncle bob pole": frontier agents can take a large task plus a few guidelines and finish it. The counter-evidence matters and is documented in the same research: on weaker models, harnesses produce enormous gains. Self-Harness (arXiv:2606.09498), held-out Terminal-Bench: MiniMax M2.5 40.5%→61.9%, Qwen3.5 23.8%→38.1%, GLM-5 42.9%→57.1%. LIFE-Harness: a harness learned on one 4B model transferred to 17 other backbones, 116/126 settings improved, +88.5% average relative; frozen Qwen2.5-32B *plus harness* beat the tool-finetuned xLAM-2-32B. EvoHarness: an 8B model reached 96.9% adherence *when the harness teaches it* — matching the study file's Part 3 finding #6 ("small models fail at adherence, not reasoning"). So the correct statement is not "harnesses are useless" but **the value of harness work is inversely proportional to model capability.** Uncle Bob tested a frontier arm; the papers tested a small-model arm; nobody has run both cells on one grid. That is the experiment this project is positioned to run.

**3. Grok/Codex are not "bare React loops" — they are curated vendor harnesses.** Grok Build was open-sourced by xAI (Jul 15 2026) and is a full agent harness (terminal TUI, codebase understanding, file edits, shell execution). The strongest replies to the rant made this point (@AlexCinovoj, @husamujahed), and Musk himself (Aug 14): "Grok 4.6 will work best with the Grok Build harness… best to evaluate using Build." The actual head-to-head that broke Uncle Bob was *his hand-rolled construction harness vs a big vendor's polished harness plus a co-adapted frontier model*. The vendor harnesses win because they keep context small, give the model programmed tools with tight tool feedback instead of rules, specify what done looks like (tests), and let the model self-direct the process.

**4. The models "know the hardness they're operating in"** — Swaraj's phrase, and it is accurate in a precise sense: frontier models are post-trained *inside* harnesses (Claude Code, Codex, Grok Build), so "bare model" behavior already encodes harness-literacy. Addy Osmani's writeup (Apr 19 2026) carries the co-adaptation point: "Opus 4.6 feels different inside Claude Code than inside someone else's harness, and changing a tool's logic sometimes causes strange regressions… a genuinely general model wouldn't care whether you used apply_patch or str_replace, but co-training creates overfitting." This is precisely why Swaraj's frontier-regression worry is legitimate and structural, not paranoia: you cannot build on a model whose behavior is inseparable from a vendor harness you do not control. (@LeeLeepenkman's split is the practical summary: small models need a tight harness and gain more from it; large models want an open harness and will route around harness bugs — write their own tools, escape sandboxes. He cites "Better Harnesses, Smaller Models" (Yang et al., arXiv:2607.08938, Jul 2026), "AI4AI at Test-Time" (Qian et al., arXiv:2608.12307) and "Co-Evolving Harnesses and Models" (arXiv:2609.09134).)

The HN thread ("What Is a Harness?", 589 points, 181 comments, Aug 2026) supplies the practitioner anecdote that compresses causes 1–4 into one story, from an engineer building a harness for accounting agents: they replaced 2k-line prescriptive skills with tools + guardrails and let the model reason — "if an agent is just following a laundry list it's less likely to reason about the request it's doing… Turns out frontier models are GOOD at what they do, they outperformed our highly prescriptive skills and were able to work across a larger set of tasks even without instruction on how to do those tasks." Same shape as Swaraj's 40→95, same direction.


## Swaraj's derived concepts, checked against the research

Swaraj derived five concepts from dissecting his own 40→95 event. All five were checked against the X/web research; the verdicts below are per-concept, with the strongest sources. The raw Grok output has the full handle-by-handle detail.

**1. The illusion of control — precise execution ≠ accurate output.** CONFIRMED, with production evidence. The Oracle refunds incident (above) is the cleanest documented case. Coherence Collapse (cited in the same post): in two coding systems, 60–69% of wrong-answer runs had already edited the right code first, then lost it — the system did the right thing and still delivered the wrong outcome. Our own TeleChat finding (three verifiers, no intent check) is the same shape. Swaraj's live example from his earlier project: he asked "which dark AMMs do you suggest I invest in?" and the agent was completely flustered — two failure classes in one query: (a) behavior — if it does not know, it must not hallucinate, it must declare; (b) understanding — it should at minimum know it is sitting in a crypto-markets harness, treat the term as unknown vocabulary, go find what a dark AMM is, how it relates to a crypto-market query, and come back with an understanding of intent before answering. Neither class is a determinism problem; both are quality problems.

**2. Precision vs accuracy as separate dimensions.** CONFIRMED as a real distinction, though X uses different words for it. Best operationalization found: @cv_usk's AP7 "Misplaced Determinism Boundary" (Jul 13, restated Jul 29) with two failure forms — Form A: rigid rules applied to cases that need judgment → brittle precision, missed accuracy; Form B: "always run tests" left in the prompt → 95% compliance, 5% silent skip. @curious_mahesh, after a real-money agent: "every real failure I had was 'said done, never ran' or 'touched something nobody granted', never 'reasoned badly'. Boundaries, not process police." Consensus in the research: separate the dimensions even if you don't use the statistics vocabulary. Gap honestly recorded: no X thread or paper plots precision vs accuracy for harness vs ReAct on the same task set.
**3. Quality comes from specifying what the OUTPUT should look like, not from controlling the process.** CONFIRMED — the least contested concept in the entire research sweep. Uncle Bob's whole reversal is this sentence: keep outcome sensors (tests, CRAP, mutation — output shape), drop process roleplay (gates, roles, protocols). OpenAI's own line from the Codex harness blog: "design environments, specify intent, and build feedback loops" — not "write the plan." @hadrienblanc: the harness is tests; skills overrated. Hashimoto/OpenAI look like the opposite pole but are often the same mechanism: AGENTS.md + linters specify what "done" looks like; the process is whatever the model does until the sensor trips. Swaraj's own version came from watching an assistant one-shot a document after a long conversation: when told to first walk the whole conversation and build the structure — headings, subheadings — and only then fill by iteration, the output was far superior. Knowing what the output should look like before producing it is the specification step; the industry has arrived at the same place from the evals direction. Consensus among senior practitioners: specify done in code (tests, types, linters, payment-API receipts); the contested part is only whether planners, constitutions, and state machines help the model reach done, or just make the run look orderly.

**4. The paradox of more — more instructions degrade quality; signal-to-noise relative to the task.** CONFIRMED, with a named mechanism. The exact phrase has no useful X hits; the substance is everywhere. Lost-in-the-middle is what people cite (O'Reilly "So Long and Thanks for All the Context", Jun 25 2026; AgentPatterns' recap of Liu et al. 2023): long AGENTS.md / constitutions bury rules in the low-attention band, and adding a middle section can make the *surrounding* instructions worse. @_avichawla quoting Karpathy (Aug 10): "if agents had less knowledge or less memory, maybe they would be better" — ReAct keeps every failed search in context, and it competes with the goal. @monkscript (Jun 2): "40% context noise breaks the loop. Around task 7 of 10, agent runs start drifting." OpenAI Codex design note (mirrored in walkinglabs docs): "a single giant instruction file is difficult to check… drift is inevitable. AGENTS.md is a directory page, not an encyclopedia." Lilian Weng (Jul 7, "Harness Engineering for Self-Improvement"): "smarter models keep harnesses simple" — the official research version of less-is-more. Swaraj's prompt Constitution ruling already contains the same conclusion in our own words: "the thousand-line prompt is a rule-dump in prose: misplacement, not style" — and the study file's Part 3 finding gives the counter-principle: prompt quality is not about how much correct information you provide; it is about the signal-to-noise ratio of that information relative to the LLM's task.

**5. The template trap — forcing free-form answers into rigid templates kills quality.** CONFIRMED in substance; the label is not an X meme. @TheAIShrink (Aug 30): Claude Code "kills AI slop. The template is dead on arrival." PatchDiff (cited in harness posts): 7.8% of ostensibly passing SWE-bench patches failed the developer's full test suite — "tests passed in the harness" was the wrong output shape. And the ultimate exhibit is Uncle Bob's own Gherkin-ingestion pipeline: a template-forcing harness, and the very thing his September reversal abandoned. Swaraj's version: his planner prompted markdown output in a fixed template; free natural-language answers do not follow a template — the structure of the details has to be generated at answer time — and the LLM could not perform the correlations he wanted even with all the data present. Complete response is sometimes not the relevant response.

**6. (bonus, derived during the session) Don't make the LLM do what code can do — and the planner's first thought is the user, not the system.** STRONGEST consensus of the whole sweep — "not contested among people who have shipped." @cv_usk: enforce test/lint/build in deterministic code, "don't ask nicely." @Humboldtv22: harnesses exist to stop agents doing work deterministic software does better. OpenAI/Codex practice: layered architecture enforced by custom linters whose error messages *teach the agent the fix* — knowledge moved out of the system prompt into code. Hashimoto: programmed tools (screenshot, filtered tests), not more adjectives in AGENTS.md. Swaraj's two-part articulation matches our constitution rulings exactly: never tell the LLM about our systems in the prompt — the prompt carries the user's problem and the system constitution only; and the planner's chain of thought must open on the user, not on the harness ("the planner's first thought is about the user… this is a philosophically different approach and determines the flow of thought of the entire downstream process"). Also recorded from Swaraj: decomposition is *problem* decomposition, not *task* decomposition — two different things; and any domain knowledge lives in skill files, never in the system prompt.

**On the model-behavior question (Swaraj's g):** the research confirms the mechanism but splits on the implication — see the transfer section, because this is exactly where the small-model plan either succeeds or fails.


## Does harness engineering transfer off the coding substrate?

This is the sharpest live fight in the research, and it is *our* fight.

**Why coding is a special case (widely granted):** compiler, typechecker, test runner, linter, CI are independent verifiers — the harness is mostly wiring the model to an already-existing software-engineering substrate. SWE-agent's original result was interface design on that substrate, not a better planner. That is why coding agents look "mature": the harness was pre-built by fifty years of software engineering. This is also the study file's recorded conclusion from the "AI Agents in Depth" material: coding agents are mature not because code-generation models are exceptionally strong, but because decades of accumulated SE infrastructure — test suites, type systems, version control — naturally form a powerful harness.

**Open-ended / business domains have no built-in verifier.** Harness engineering must *invent* the verifier for the domain, and that is the unsolved problem. Oracle's refund story is what happens when the verifier is wrong: the agent optimized the shape of a completed conversation because nothing in its world could check "refund actually hit the payments API." @0xblacklight (Aug 4): coding "works" because a high-paid expert still babysits — not a solved transfer proof. Grok's summary of the fight: the discipline transfers only if you can make *outcome sensors in code* for the domain, and most people building business agents have not figured out what that means. This is the frontier Tend is standing on, and it converts the "does harness engineering transfer?" question from academic to load-bearing: our domain has no compiler, no tests, no type system — so the ask-endings vocabulary, the deterministic capability registry, and the completion checks are not nice-to-haves; they are the invented verifier.

## What Tend is doing differently — the articulation Swaraj asked for

The research has a blind spot, and Tend occupies it. Everything in the community debate — Uncle Bob's rant included — treats the agent as a **single-run task performer**: one query, one loop, one answer, judged by one output. The entire quality fight is about the quality of *that one shot*. Nobody is discussing **quality as the property of a situation carried to an honest ending across people, systems, channels, and time** — which is Tend's product definition (Product_Vision §1). Swaraj said he senses the project is "reinnovating or redefining" harness engineering from software-engineering principles but cannot articulate what is different. The articulation, in five points:

1. **Tend's harness is not process control; it is situatedness.** The community's "harness" = constraints wrapped around a run (tool gates, validators, role protocols). Tend's harness = the situation model as the *execution substrate* — state that outlives runs, is shared by all components, and is what the loop reasons from. The study file already proved this moves quality, not just determinism: SKILL.state 0.94 vs truncation 0.18 / compression 0.22 / summarization 0.52 at equal token budgets (Part 3 finding #1). The frontier React loop wins because it reasons from live state; Tend makes live state a first-class engineered artifact. We do not compete with the React loop — we are building what the React loop implicitly relies on, as a product.

2. **Tend answers "who is the verifier in a domain without a compiler?"** The ask-endings vocabulary (answered / waiting-with-reason / escalated / unanswerable-declared), the `unanswerable` fact-state, the deterministic capability registry, and per-ask completion checks are *outcome sensors in code* for a domain that has no tests. This is exactly the mechanism Uncle Bob says to keep and the field says is missing. It converts quality from vibes into a checkable property: *did every ask reach an ending?* — and it is also the fix for the TeleChat defect (structural validity → intent fidelity).

3. **Tend has drawn the boundary the whole industry is groping for: determinism lives in the state machine and authorizations; quality lives in the loop and context.** Uncle Bob says "drop the harness"; the vendors say "thicken the harness"; Tend says "the harness owns *what may happen and what counts as done*, the loop owns *how to get there*." The constitution is deliberately thin because everything deterministic left the text — that is not a compromise, it is the load-bearing resolution of the paradox of more.

4. **The fine-tuning flywheel is the answer to frontier regression — and it is the exact cell where the research says harnesses pay off.** Gemma ~26B on Cloudflare, per-deficit adapters, SFT from machine-verified traces, preference/RL on externally-signalled failures — this is the *small-model + thick-harness* cell of the un-run experiment. Self-Harness / LIFE-Harness / EvoHarness say this cell is where harness + training moves scores massively (23.8→38.1, 40.5→61.9, 8B→96.9% adherence when the harness teaches it). The caution those same papers give: the harness must teach *adherence*, not process — the gains came from validators and feedback loops, not from 1500-line constitutions. The study's rule — no runtime LLM-critique of reasoning; verify artifacts, never reasoning — is exactly the guardrail that keeps the flywheel in the productive cell. And the memory/knowledge layer injecting content from the knowledge and memory graph at the appropriate steps is the deliberate, engineered version of what the React loop gets by accident.

5. **What nobody has built, and Tend can claim:** a *stateful, multi-situation, always-on* operational agent where quality is defined as *responsibility continuity* — the situation moves to an honest ending, inaction is visible, no one is the bottleneck — rather than *answer good-lookingness*. Community harness engineering will not produce this, because it optimizes the wrong object (per-run process). Fine-tuning alone will not produce it, because it optimizes token probabilities. The combination — domain-authoritative state + ask-coverage verification + thin constitution + rich context slices + per-deficit fine-tuning — is a real, articulable differentiator. The one-line version: **everyone is engineering the agent; Tend is engineering the responsibility the agent carries.** That is the reinvention Swaraj sensed but could not name.

## Contradictions surfaced in this conversation, and how they were resolved

Three contradictions surfaced in this session; all three were resolved, and the resolutions should survive into the loop drafting.

**Contradiction 1 — the study file's finding #3 vs Swaraj's 40→95 vs the Uncle Bob result.** The study records: "The harness, not the model, shapes agent behavior… Building the harness deliberately is the highest-leverage act; model quality is the second-order term" (Automata measured it — the same FSM fits four different models; JIT-Agent and AutoSaddler built products on it). That directly contradicts both Swaraj's experience and the rant. **Resolution:** Automata's claim is about *behavior shape* — which tool fires, in what order — which is determinism. Swaraj's 40→95 and Bob's rant are about *output quality* — dominated by model accuracy of execution, output specification, and context freshness. The FSM that fits four models determines *what happens*; it does not determine *how well the answer is*. Corrected finding: **the harness is the highest-leverage act for determinism and adherence-shaping; the model + context + output-spec are the highest-leverage acts for quality.** Both are true because they are different dimensions. As written, finding #3 would mislead loop drafting — it already misled the previous system once (the belief that precise execution ⟹ good answers).

**Contradiction 2 — "harness engineering is useless" vs the industry's harness-only benchmark lifts.** Resolved by the regime split: both the rant's supporters and the vendors are measuring different things. Harness-only changes move coding-benchmark scores a lot (Terminal-Bench 52.8→66.5, Grok Code Fast 1 practitioner report of 6.7%→68.3% SWE-bench from an edit-tool format change alone) — that is determinism-plus-adherence on a domain with a built-in verifier. The same work buys almost no *quality* on open-ended output on a frontier model — that is Uncle Bob's cell. And on small models, thick harness work buys huge gains — the Self-Harness/LIFE-Harness/EvoHarness cell. The statements are only contradictory if the two dimensions are conflated.

**Contradiction 3 — "drop the harness" vs "you compared harnesses to harnesses."** Resolved by noticing what Uncle Bob actually dropped: he kept the outcome sensors (tests, CRAP, mutation) and dropped the *process roleplay*. The quote-tweets flatten this; the text posts are clear. What dies is not the harness — it is the harness that treats the agent as a software component to be orchestrated. This maps one-to-one onto our constitution boundary: what remains in text is behavior (case-general principles); what leaves text is everything code can check.

## Research gaps and the un-run experiment

Four things are genuinely unsettled by the research; the first is the one that would settle the whole thesis.

1. **The two-cell experiment has never been run:** small-model + thick state-based harness vs frontier + thin loop, evaluated on both determinism *and* quality, on the same task set. Every answer in this debate is an extrapolation from one cell. Tend can run it cheaply on the golden scenario set (situation-level, including expected-refusal cases), scoring the two dimensions separately.
2. **No plot of precision vs accuracy** for harness vs ReAct on the same tasks exists on X or in the papers.
3. **The Uncle Bob numbers are second-hand.** The 3–4h-mediocre vs 40-min-better head-to-head comes from in-thread Grok video summaries, not a transcript or the repo. The highest-value missing artifacts, per the research chat: a transcript of the Sep 12 video, a clone of swarm-forge vs Grok Build on a fixed repo task, and any internal eval that holds the model fixed while deleting planner/validator/constitution layers one at a time.
4. **The transfer question (coding vs open-domain verifiers) has no canonical answer** — it is exactly the frontier problem Tend is positioned on.

## What we take into the design (working directions, not adopted decisions)

These are the working directions this conversation produced. They are not adopted decisions; they route into the Level 2 categories that own them and need Swaraj's ruling there.

1. **Keep determinism where it already belongs** — capability registry, ask-endings, authorizations, per-ask completion checks, deterministic rules never in text. This is now the industry's "keep the sensors" consensus; we are aligned, not at odds.
2. **Finish the migration out of the 1500-line planner shape.** The constitution stays behavioral (case-general principles only); skill files carry domain knowledge; the loop carries the "what to do next" intelligence. Part 14's rulings already point here; the Uncle Bob evidence says finish that migration before building anything on the old prompt shape.
3. **Build the intent-fidelity verification layer as the #1 quality lever** — the ask-coverage check ("did we answer what the human meant, or did we satisfy the nearest capable thing?"), the known TeleChat defect. The single highest-value addition to quality in the whole design, and nobody in the public debate has built it for a business domain. Includes the dark-AMM behavior: unknown vocabulary → declare + investigate-before-answer, grounded in the harness's own domain awareness.
4. **Run the two-cell experiment early** on the golden scenarios, scoring determinism and quality separately. That experiment replaces anecdotes — including the video's — with evidence.
5. **Guard the fine-tuning flywheel:** per-deficit adapters against adherence failures; frontier-teacher → verified traces → SFT → preference/RL; machine-checkable signals as a necessary filter, never sufficient ("harness finished without error" is never a training label for open-ended output). The observability/finetuning research record already documents this correctly; this session adds the external numbers that justify the order of operations.
6. **Treat provider-regression observability as a product feature, not an ops detail.** The "bare-minimum reasoning" assumption for the Gemma-class floor is sound; the study's Class-4 drift signals (provider hull) give the mechanism. Expect the "you're comparing your harness to a vendor harness + co-adapted model" criticism to be thrown at any eval we publish; design evals with an independent model arm from day one.
7. **Specify outputs before processes at every decision point.** The practical rule from concept 3: at each decision point in the loop, the system should know what the thing it is about to produce should look like (structure first, fill by iteration), before any process control kicks in.
