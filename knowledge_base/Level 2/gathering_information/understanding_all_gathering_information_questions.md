# Understanding all the Gathering Information questions

## Why this document exists

The eight Gathering Information questions are easy to treat as eight unrelated checklist items.

That would make the category harder to think about.

They are really eight views of one problem:

> How does Tend move from an incomplete situation model to a current and traceable context that can support the next safe decision?

This document is a map for holding the whole category in mind.

It is not a decision record for the questions we have not discussed yet.

So far, we have discussed the first question in detail. The others are still open, even though we can already understand what they are asking and how they connect.

## The eight questions in plain language

### 1. What information is required before making a decision?

This asks:

> Which claims, context or evidence could change the next safe step?

It is not asking Tend to fill every empty field.

It is asking us to distinguish information that matters from information that is merely available.

This is the starting question because we cannot decide who to ask, where to look or what to retrieve until we know what matters.

Our current working answer is that required information is decision-relative. It includes claims that are missing, unverified, stale or conflicting when those conditions could change the next step.

### 2. How do we know which actor owns each piece of information?

This asks:

> Who is responsible for knowing, maintaining, confirming or correcting this information?

The actor might be:

- The customer or prospect
- An employee
- A business owner
- A business system
- An external partner
- A communication platform

Ownership and source are not the same.

An employee may provide a claim while the business owns the policy. A payment provider may own a transaction while the business system records its status. The customer may be authoritative about their experience without owning the business record.

This question begins after Question 1 has identified what information matters.

### 3. How do we discover where information lives?

This asks:

> Where can the information actually be found or obtained?

Information may live in:

- A business system
- A previous conversation
- A previous situation version
- An employee’s knowledge
- The customer’s experience
- An external partner’s record
- A policy or document

The owner may not be the location.

For example, an employee may own the responsibility for answering a question, but the information may live in a business system they use.

Question 2 helps us identify who is responsible. Question 3 helps us discover where the information can be obtained.

### 4. How do we prioritise which information to retrieve first?

This asks:

> When several pieces of information are missing, which one should we pursue first?

The order may matter because:

- One result may determine which other information is relevant
- One source may be time-sensitive
- One fact may resolve several unknowns
- One missing answer may block all safe progress
- One actor may take longer to respond
- One piece of information may have greater consequence if wrong

This question depends on the first three questions. We need to know what matters, who can provide it and where it can be found before we can decide what to pursue first.

### 5. How do we avoid retrieving unnecessary information?

This asks:

> What should Tend deliberately leave uncollected for now?

This is the counterweight to Question 1.

Without it, “required information” can quietly become “everything that might be interesting.”

Information may be unnecessary because:

- It cannot change the next decision
- It belongs to another situation
- It creates noise
- It delays a simple answer
- It exposes information to an actor who does not need it
- Another claim would answer the question more directly

Question 1 tells us what may matter. Question 5 protects the boundary around that requirement.

### 6. How do we know when information is sufficiently current?

This asks:

> Does this information still describe the situation now?

Information can exist and come from a reliable source while still being too old for the current decision.

Examples:

- An order was in transit yesterday but arrived today
- A refund was processing earlier but is now completed or reversed
- An employee was responsible earlier but is now unavailable
- A business policy changed after the previous conversation
- A promised delivery date has already passed

Question 6 gives time a role in the meaning of information.

It connects especially closely to Questions 3, 7 and 8 because source location, delayed information and changing information all affect currentness.

### 7. How do we handle information that arrives gradually?

This asks:

> What should happen when information arrives in pieces rather than in one complete answer?

Examples include:

- A customer answers only part of a clarification
- An employee provides some context and needs to check the rest
- A business system returns only part of what was requested
- A partner acknowledges a request but has not finished investigating

This question is about partial progress.

The situation should not pretend to be complete, but it should not become useless merely because one answer is still pending.

Tend needs to make visible:

- What has arrived
- What remains unknown
- Who is expected to respond
- Why the situation is waiting
- What will happen when the next information arrives

### 8. How do we handle information that changes while a situation is being analysed?

This asks:

> What happens when information gathered earlier becomes outdated, corrected or replaced while the situation is still open?

Examples include:

- A delivery changes from in transit to delivered
- A refund changes from processing to completed
- An employee becomes unavailable after being assigned work
- A new message changes what the customer means
- A business policy changes while an approval is pending

This question connects Gathering to the latest known state, conflicts, correction and versioning.

The earlier information should not be erased. A meaningful change creates a new complete situation-model version with a reason explaining what changed.

## How the eight questions depend on each other

The simple path looks like this:

```text
What matters?
    ↓
Who owns it?
    ↓
Where does it live?
    ↓
What should we retrieve first?
    ↓
What should we leave alone?
    ↓
Is what we found current?
    ↓
What has arrived so far?
    ↓
What changed while we were working?
    ↓
Updated situation model
```

But this is a loop rather than a one-time sequence.

A new source result may change what information matters. Discovering the owner may reveal a better source. A source becoming unavailable may change the priority. A new status may make an earlier request unnecessary. A conflict may reveal that the message was routed to the wrong situation.

The answer to one question can therefore send us back to an earlier question.

## Two useful groups

The questions can also be held as two groups.

### The “what should we pursue?” group

Questions 1 to 5 are about selecting and controlling gathering work:

- What matters?
- Who can provide it?
- Where is it?
- What comes first?
- What should we not retrieve?

This group prevents Gathering from becoming either random or unlimited.

### The “what is happening over time?” group

Questions 6 to 8 are about the changing state of information:

- Is it current?
- Has all of it arrived?
- Did it change while we were working?

This group prevents Tend from treating information as static.

The two groups cannot be separated completely. A changed source can alter what should be gathered. A partial response can change which actor should be contacted next.

## The refund example across all eight questions

The customer requested a refund. The business record later said that the refund was completed. The customer then said that the money had not arrived.

Question 1 asks what we actually need to know. This may include what “completed” means, whether the record is current, whether the provider settled the refund and whether the customer is referring to the same refund.

Question 2 asks who owns each claim. The customer owns the report of their experience. The business owns the customer relationship. The payment provider may own the transaction process.

Question 3 asks where those claims can be found. They may be in the refund record, the payment provider’s record, the earlier conversation or the customer’s reply.

Question 4 asks which information to pursue first. Confirming the refund identity and current status may come before asking someone to take corrective action.

Question 5 asks what not to retrieve. Unrelated orders, unrelated payment history and unrelated customer conversations should remain outside this gathering pass.

Question 6 asks whether the business record still describes the current state.

Question 7 asks how to represent the period while the payment provider has acknowledged the request but has not finished checking it.

Question 8 asks what happens if the refund moves from processing to completed, or from completed to reversed, while the situation is open.

The example shows why these questions are connected. They are not eight independent fields on a form.

## How this category relates to Understanding

Understanding works from the information already available and creates the current situation model.

Gathering uses that model to find, check and refresh decision-relevant information.

If Gathering discovers that the earlier understanding was wrong, it does not silently replace it. The situation moves forward through a new version with a visible reason.

Understanding does not need to wait until all facts are known.

Gathering does not need to reinterpret the actor’s message from scratch every time.

The fuller relationship is described in [`How Understanding and Gathering Information work together`](relationship_between_understanding_and_gathering_information.md).

## How this category relates to the other categories

### Trust and Evidence

Gathering obtains claims and records their sources, timing and versions.

Trust and Evidence examines how authoritative or reliable those claims are.

### Decision Making

Gathering makes the current information state and remaining gaps visible.

Decision Making determines whether the state is sufficient for a particular action and whether policy permits it.

### Human Collaboration

Gathering may ask an employee or external partner for information.

Human Collaboration determines ownership, routing, handoff and escalation.

### Coordination and Time

Gathering identifies what is waiting and why.

Coordination and Time determine how waiting, deadlines and forgotten work behave.

### Memory and Knowledge

Gathering may use previous conversations and situation history.

Memory and Knowledge determines what should become reusable business knowledge and what should remain situation-specific history.

## What we have and have not decided

We have worked through the first question:

> Required information is the decision-relevant set of claims, context and evidence whose absence, staleness, incorrectness or conflict could change the safe next step.

We have also established the relationship between Gathering and Understanding, including references, provenance, versioning and actor-neutral conversations.

We have not yet answered the remaining seven questions.

Their meaning is clear enough to discuss, but their conceptual solutions still need their own conversations.

## How to use this map

When we begin Question 2, we should keep this whole map in view but discuss only one question at a time.

For each question, we can:

- Start with what the question means
- Connect it to the previous questions
- Use one shared example
- Bring in research and constraints
- Challenge weak assumptions
- Decide what belongs inside this question
- Leave neighbouring questions open where necessary

The individual question documents will hold the detailed conversations and working decisions.

This document exists so that those individual discussions do not become disconnected from one another.
