## The perceiver's machinery — why Krug and Tufte hold

**The problem that births it.** Krug priced seconds, Tufte priced arrangement — but *what* spends them? The eye and brain run on fixed, measurable rules. Known, a screen's cost can be predicted before anyone uses it.

**Part A — grouping happens before thinking (Gestalt, UP):** Proximity p.160 (nearness = one unit) · Similarity p.184 (alike = one kind) · Closure p.34 (the eye completes shapes) · Common Fate p.40 (moving together = one group) · Uniform Connectedness p.200 (a link makes a hard group) · Figure–ground pp.80–81 (front/back split; ambiguity is unstable tax) · Prägnanz, the simplicity law, p.120 (the simplest reading wins).

**Part B — two metered costs.** Hick's Law, UP p.102: decision time grows with the number of *meaningful* choices — keep the live option set small. Fitts's Law, UP p.82: pointing is movement — big and near is fast and forgiving; tiny and far is slow and missed.

**Part C — the aesthetic-usability mechanism (UP p.18, now the mechanism, not the citation).** Beauty is judged *before* interaction, and the judgment colors the patience after it: a confident-looking screen earns forward motion and less defensive scanning. "Pretty" is usability physics, not luxury — and a broken pretty surface is the worst false signifier, because it bought trust with the eye.

**Tend (example only):** proximity welds the state word to its situation (an accidental gap files the value under the wrong neighbor — Krug's scanner still misreads it); Hick's shrinks each moment's live options to the state's relevant few ("what's there is right, nothing else there at all"); Fitts prices the need-me action big and near; beauty buys the forward motion the morning loop runs on.

**Teaching correction (2026-09-14) — Swaraj's words, standing rule.** The first telling of this unit hid the idea under long words ("metered," "Prägnanz," "cost curves") — Swaraj re-pasted the constitution: the word usage was causing friction. Rule confirmed: canon names may be *named*, but every lesson must also exist in plain words a first-time reader follows with zero decoding. One sentence, one idea. (Also corrected: Hick's UP p.102, Fitts's UP p.82 — not 103/84 as first cited.)

## Rams — ten principles, one idea

**The problem that births it.** Dieter Rams, Braun design director, 1960s–70s (radios, calculators, kitchen machines): the world covered products in fake wood, chrome, decoration. His answer: strip until only what works is left — then make *that* beautiful. **Design should be honest, and as little as possible.** ("Less, but better.")

**The ten, one line each:** 1. New — may try new things. 2. Useful — must actually work; pretty never covers useless. 3. Pretty — beauty is part of the job. 4. Understandable — see what it does without a manual. 5. Quiet — doesn't shout; the work is the focus. 6. Honest — doesn't pretend (fake wood on plastic; a button that looks pressable and does nothing). 7. Lasting — built to be kept. 8. Thorough — details done; nothing half-thought. 9. Lean — as little material as possible. 10. As little as possible — whatever can be removed, remove.

**Echoes:** rule 6 (honest) ↔ false signifier ↔ anti-slop blacklist (item 9) — slop is dishonesty, not bad taste. Rule 10 ↔ H8 ↔ Tufte signal-to-noise ↔ Refactoring UI "de-emphasize." Rule 4 ↔ conceptual model. Rule 5 (quiet) ↔ the exception view (one shouts, forty stay quiet).

**Swaraj's contradiction — and its resolution (the cake question).** "How can it be pretty while as-little-as-possible? CSS *is* decoration on HTML; icing doesn't change taste; yet playfulness is appreciated." Resolution, as taught: (1) "as little as possible" ≠ few pixels — *nothing without a job*; beauty = the structure's own truth done with care, not added costume. (2) Even decoration is allowed *when it communicates*: icing says *occasion*; a Spiderman cake is a message — a lie only when it pretends (fake wood = lie; gripping leather = honest craft). (3) The X hover character and the YouTube subscribe pulse are *earned moments inside a quiet system* — restraint resting, delight on invitation (UP Highlighting pp.108–109: everything glowing = nothing lit); the pulse is feedback + pointing, pure function. (4) There is no undecorated form — unstyled HTML is the browser's *random* decoration; CSS is where pixels *decide their meaning* (spacing=grouping, weight=hierarchy, shadow=signifier, hover=feedback, one accent=the important word). The Win95 form isn't minimal — it's *equal-noise*. **Pretty and as-little-as-possible are the same act.** (The door to Module 2; Module 4 is its other half.)

## Emotional design (Norman) — the three levels of feeling

**The problem that births it.** The canon treated the user as a *thinker*. But people *feel* products — and feeling is not one thing. Three levels, always on at once:

1. **Visceral — the gut.** Instant, pre-use reaction: look, shape, color. The Aesthetic-Usability figure (UP p.18); Krug's 3-second scan is a visceral event. Cannot be argued with — only designed for.
2. **Behavioral — the use.** Does it work, is it easy, does it respond. The entire canon so far lives here.
3. **Reflective — the story.** Conscious, after-the-fact meaning: identity, pride, memory, trust. Where the product gets *recommended* — nobody tells friends "the mapping was good"; they tell the story. Times: before / during / after.

**The rule.** A product *feels* good only when all three are designed. Usable + ugly + meaningless = unloved.

**Tend (example only):** the landing page is visceral (serious/calm/trustworthy, or gimmicky — decided before any action); the morning loop is behavioral (every graded heuristic); the six-month story ("it runs my business") is reflective — and the honest acknowledgment, repeated, builds it one truthful moment at a time. **Trust is a reflective-level building material.**

**Echoes:** visceral ↔ machinery + Aesthetic-Usability p.18. Behavioral ↔ five failures + ten heuristics (the canon, *named*). Reflective ↔ conceptual model (the story stays true — lying surfaces poison trust slowly).
